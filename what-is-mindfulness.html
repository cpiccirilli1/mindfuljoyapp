What Is Mindfulness?

A beginner’s guide · Updated November 8, 2026

You have probably had the moment: you reach the bottom of a page and realize you took in none of it, or you finish your morning beverage without really tasting it. Mindfulness is the practice of noticing moments like that, and of being more present for the ones that follow. This guide explains what it is, what it is not, and a few simple ways to begin.

Definition

Mindfulness is noticing what is happening around and within you without judging the experience. It means letting yourself notice what is in your experience right now. Take the morning beverage again: you can feel the warm cup, the press of the ceramic against your lip, the taste of the brew, and the steam as you drink. These are simple, everyday parts of drinking something warm, but how often do you notice them while they are happening? Being fully in the present moment can make it feel richer, and that is where mindfulness comes in.

What it is not

Mindfulness can bring richness to a moment, but several common misconceptions can stand in the way of practicing it.

  1. You do not have to empty your mind. This misconception comes up most often when people talk about meditation. Mindfulness is not about having zero thoughts. It is about being aware of how you think, feel, and react to the thoughts you have. For example, you are drinking your beverage when you suddenly remember the laundry you need to do later. Notice that thought, then return your attention to your drink.
  2. Relaxing is not the same as mindfulness. We are often told that a moment of mindfulness is just a break. It can be pleasant to set your stress aside, but the goal is to be more present, and being present takes work.
  3. You do not need to hold any belief. Many religious and spiritual traditions teach mindfulness, but you can practice it without belonging to any of them. It is something you already do naturally in everyday life, and you do not need a new religion or a new way of being.
  4. It is not a cure. Many mental health professionals teach mindfulness, but it is not a cure-all. It can motivate you to invest in your well-being, but it will not solve the problems you find along the way.

Five ways to practice

1. Pause for one minute of breathing

Stop what you are doing and follow three to five slow breaths. Notice the air entering and leaving, and the rise and fall of your chest or belly. When your attention wanders, bring it back. If you would like a pace to follow, the Breathe tab guides you with a slowly growing and shrinking circle.

2. Sit for five minutes

Choose a comfortable position, set the meditation timer for five minutes so you do not have to watch the clock, and rest your attention on your breath. Each time you notice you have drifted into thoughts, gently return. That moment of noticing and returning is the exercise, and it is not a failure.

3. Scan your body

Starting at your feet and moving slowly up to your head, bring your attention to each area in turn and notice what is there: warmth, pressure, tingling, or nothing in particular. You are observing, not trying to change anything. It works well lying down or sitting, and takes five to fifteen minutes. A timer with a soft end bell lets you do it without watching the clock.

4. Walk with attention

Walk slowly, even if only across a room, and feel your feet meeting the ground. Notice the shift of weight with each step. The Sit + walk option in the timer, found in the session length menu, lets you alternate sitting and walking in one session.

5. Choose an everyday moment

Pick one routine activity and give it your full attention: the first sip of coffee, washing dishes, brushing your teeth. Notice the temperature, the sounds, the smells and how your body moves. Doing this once a day is a gentle way to bring the practice into ordinary life.

Common obstacles

  • “I can’t stop thinking.” Nobody can. Noticing a thought is already mindfulness. Label it “thinking” and return to your breath.
  • “I get restless.” Try a shorter session or a walking practice, and let the restlessness be something you observe.
  • “I fall asleep.” Sit upright, practice earlier in the day, or keep your eyes slightly open.
  • “I don’t have time.” One minute counts. Attach it to something you already do, like the first minute after you sit at your desk, and use the Breathe tab to keep it short.
  • “I’m bad at this.” If you noticed that you were distracted, you did it right.
